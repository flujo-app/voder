A voice from two raw sounds

At Bell Laboratories, engineer Homer Dudley explored how speech could be represented electrically. The Voder brought the synthesis side of that work to an audience: a buzzing tone and a hissing noise, shaped into speech by an operator.

A pedal controlled pitch. Ten keys shaped the frequency bands. A wrist control switched the source, while special keys helped form the brief releases of stop consonants. The machine needed a performer to make those gestures into words.

The person behind the voice

Helen Harper was one of the Voder’s celebrated operators and trained others to play it. In the surviving demonstration, “she saw me” becomes a lesson in expression: the same electrical voice could change its emphasis and turn a statement into a question.

The world of tomorrow

The Voder appeared at the 1939 New York World’s Fair and the Golden Gate International Exposition in San Francisco. It was a demonstration of what electrical synthesis could do, rather than a consumer product. The human skill behind it was part of the wonder.

THE INSTRUMENT IN FRONT OF YOU

This studio carries that idea forward. Play ten resonators by hand, or let our text-to-speech engine supply the gestures. Classic uses a fixed filter bank; Clear adds moving vowel resonances. Every sound is synthesized locally. This is a modern interpretation, not a replica of the original circuitry.
